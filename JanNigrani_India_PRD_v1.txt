Product Requirements Document
India-wide public accountability platform for elected representatives, local governments and development spending
Version 0.1  |  Research baseline: 31 August 2026  |  Status: Product discovery / PRD

MP -> MLA -> Mayor / ULB -> Gram Pradhan / Gram Panchayat
Core promise

Primary users
Citizens • journalists • researchers • civil-society groups • local watchdogs
MVP principle
Source-first, role-aware and explicit about data completeness.
Key differentiator
A national entity + geography + fund-flow graph linking fragmented Indian public systems.
Important product note: this platform must not turn “money spent in a constituency/city” into “money spent by the politician.” India’s public-finance architecture frequently assigns recommendation, sanction, execution, accounting and oversight to different actors. The product therefore separates these relationships throughout the data model and UI.

1. Executive summary
Jan Nigrani is proposed as a public accountability and development-intelligence platform for India. It aggregates publicly accessible government data into a user-friendly record of who represents a place, what public money is attached to that place, which projects were sanctioned and executed, what evidence exists, and which official or institution was actually responsible at each stage.
The initial concept resembles Govvy at the “find your representatives and understand their public record” layer, but the Indian product should be built around India’s fragmented administrative and financial architecture rather than a US-style federal-legislative model. Govvy currently combines representative discovery, bill tracking, voting records, committee assignments, campaign finance and contact tools. [1]
The research shows that many of the underlying ingredients already exist: MPLADS has constituency-, work- and GIS-oriented information; eGramSwaraj covers rural local-government profiling, planning, progress reporting, accounting, assets, geo-tagging and PFMS integration; LGD provides stable codes for states, districts, villages, panchayats, ULBs and wards; City Finance provides standardized municipal financial information; ECI exposes candidate affidavits and election results; parliamentary records support questions/debates; procurement and grievance systems add contract and responsiveness evidence. [2]-[12]
The product opportunity is not simply “collect all data”. It is to reconcile these sources into a single, transparent accountability graph and then present the results with enough context to prevent misleading conclusions.
Product thesis
Build the best public “money -> project -> place -> responsible institution -> evidence” graph for India.
Use geography as the default discovery path, because citizens think in terms of state, district, city, ward, village and constituency.
Treat role and responsibility as first-class data, not an annotation.
Show financial execution and delivery evidence separately; never collapse them into one simplistic productivity score.
Make every number traceable to its source, reporting period, last refresh and confidence level.
North-star outcome
A citizen can search for a place, understand which representatives and institutions govern it, see public funds and projects relevant to that place, compare peers on like-for-like indicators, inspect primary evidence, and report or verify discrepancies.
2. Problem statement
India has abundant public information but low interoperability between systems. A citizen may need to consult ECI for the representative, MPLADS for MP-recommended works, a state portal for MLA funds, eGramSwaraj for panchayat works, City Finance for municipal finances, a scheme-specific MIS for a sector programme, an e-procurement portal for contracts, and CAG or audit records for assurance. The data is useful individually but difficult to combine.
User pain points
“Who is responsible for this road, drain, school, water project or public facility?”
“How much money was actually allocated/released/sanctioned/spent—not just announced?”
“Is this project part of a politician’s discretionary/constituency fund, a department programme, a local-body budget, or a central scheme?”
“Why does another city/constituency appear to perform better?”
“Can I verify the official claim with a government source, photo, map or audit document?”
“The official says the work is complete. What public evidence supports that?”
Single biggest product risk
The platform can lose trust if it creates false attribution. For example, an MLA may recommend a work while a district authority sanctions it and a department or contractor executes it; a mayor may preside over a municipal corporation whose commissioner and engineering departments control execution. Even where money is genuinely associated with a jurisdiction, that does not make it personally “received” or “spent” by the elected official. MPLADS explicitly describes the MP’s role as recommendation, with the District Authority responsible for sanction, execution and completion. [3]

Procurement evidence is public for many central procurements

Attach tender/award/contract evidence to project records where IDs can be reconciled.
Grievance data is measurable

Add responsiveness/administrative-service indicators at the department/state level, but do not incorrectly attribute all grievances to politicians.
Citizen-facing election data is public

Use ECI as the canonical representative identity/election source where applicable.

10. Compare mode
Comparison is central, but naïve rankings are dangerous. The compare engine should normalize for role, population, geography, tenure length, fund eligibility and data completeness.
10.1 Compare dimensions
Financial execution: released-to-sanctioned / sanctioned-to-spent, with exact source definitions.
Project delivery: completion rate, median completion time, stalled-project rate.
Evidence coverage: % of records with primary-source documents / geotagged images.
Sector mix: roads, water, sanitation, education, health, public buildings, etc.
Legislative engagement: attendance/questions/debates/committees where comparable.
Audit outcomes: findings and recovery/follow-up status; not “guilt scores.”
Service outcomes for ULBs: published service-level benchmarks where available.
Data completeness: coverage by year/source to stop incomplete data from looking like clean performance.
10.2 Comparability rules
Only compare entities with the same role and a similar measurement definition.
Use a tenure-normalized period when comparing representatives who served different lengths.
Use per-capita/per-household/per-km metrics only when the denominator is reliable.
Allow users to turn normalization factors on/off.
Every chart has a methodology drawer.
11. Role-specific product logic
11.1 MP
Primary: MPLADS + parliamentary activity + constituency-level development context. MPLADS already exposes released funds, expenditure, recommended/completed works, district/block/village filters and GIS elements. The platform should enrich that data with representative identity, tenure, parliamentary record, project evidence and cross-scheme context. [2]
Important: MPLADS should be shown as a direct recommendation relationship—not as an MP personally spending public money. Official MPLADS guidance is explicit on this division of responsibility. [3]
11.2 MLA
The MLA layer should start with a State Scheme Registry. Each state gets a configuration describing the name of the scheme, annual entitlement, eligible work types, approval chain, implementing authority, portal URL/API/downloads, fiscal-year conventions and data fields. Delhi, Assam and Maharashtra demonstrate that the program design and stated allocation can differ by state. [13]-[15]
11.3 Mayor / ULB
Do not create a fake “mayor fund” metric. Instead show the municipal institution’s finances, grants, own revenue, expenditure, capital expenditure, service-level indicators, major projects, contracts and ward distribution. Add the mayor’s institutional role separately. City Finance already provides standardized city-level financial indicators and service-level benchmarking, with varying data coverage. [6], [7]
11.4 Gram Pradhan / Gram Panchayat
Use the Gram Panchayat as the financial institution and the pradhan as the elected representative. eGramSwaraj supports profile/election information, planning, progress reporting, accounting, assets, geo-tagging and PFMS-linked financial management. This creates an unusually strong foundation for a village development ledger. [4]
11.5 Minister
For ministers, use a portfolio model, not a personal-fund model. Show: portfolio tenure, ministry/department budget, expenditure, flagship scheme physical/financial performance, major policy decisions, parliamentary answers/questions, audits, procurement and outcomes. Clearly label all figures as “portfolio/department exposure”.

13. Data ingestion and engineering requirements
13.1 Source connectors
API connector when a stable public API exists.
Structured CSV/XLS/XLSX/PDF ingestion when no API exists.
HTML table extraction only where permitted and stable.
Document archive ingestion with page-level source references.
Manual source onboarding for high-value state portals during MVP, with an explicit maintenance owner.
13.2 Immutable source snapshots
Every imported record should be linked to a source snapshot: URL, retrieval timestamp, source title, reporting period, checksum/hash where feasible, and extraction version. This is essential because government portals change and some pages do not retain historical presentation.
13.3 Canonical IDs and entity resolution
LGD code for local governments and village mappings where applicable.
ECI election/candidate identifier where available.
Parliament/Assembly member identifier where available.
MPLADS work identifiers; state-scheme work IDs.
Tender IDs / award IDs / contract IDs.
Internal persistent IDs with alias tables for renamed places, constituencies, people and institutions.
13.4 Historical versioning
Store effective_from and effective_to for every representative-place relationship and every geography crosswalk. A user should be able to ask, “Who represented this place and what spending was recorded during 2022-24?” without retroactively applying today’s boundaries or officeholder.

15.2 Red flags
Red flags are not accusations. They identify records worth checking.
Large sanctioned amount with prolonged no-progress/stalled status.
Repeated revisions or cancellations.
Spend recorded but physical progress missing/unknown.
Conflicting completion status across official sources.
Audit finding unresolved beyond the recorded follow-up period.
Procurement award value substantially differs from project-reported cost without explanation.
Sudden project concentration in one contractor/vendor where public procurement data supports the comparison.
Very high apparent performance paired with very low underlying data coverage.
16. Citizen participation and evidence
16.1 Citizen report
Citizens should be able to submit a project observation without editing the government record. Example: “Government source says completed; photo taken 28 Aug 2026 appears incomplete.”
Location capture (optional), timestamp, photos/video, description.
Structured issue types: not found, incomplete, poor quality, duplicate, wrong location, different work, unsafe/damaged.
Evidence is displayed as a separate layer and cannot overwrite official data.
Moderation queue for spam, defamation, personal data and political campaigning.
Users can request a correction to source-derived data.
16.2 Community verification
Introduce a later “Verify this work” flow where multiple independent local observations can increase evidence confidence. Never turn likes/comments into factual truth; they are signals, not verification.
17. Audit and assurance layer
The platform should build a “Source Lens” into every important number. CAG’s local-body audit ecosystem demonstrates that financial and implementation weaknesses can exist even when a project is technically recorded as completed. [17]-[19]
CAG report link -> finding -> affected institution -> affected year -> affected theme.
AuditOnline / social-audit record where publicly available.
Recovery/action-taken status where source data permits.
No legal conclusion; use the source’s terminology and quote minimally.
18. Procurement intelligence
CPPP publishes tender enquiries, corrigenda and award-of-contract details for central government organizations. [20] State procurement systems vary. The product should therefore treat procurement as an evidence enrichment layer and use confidence-based entity matching.
Match project title/location/work department to tender title and tender ID.
Show tender value, award value, vendor and dates where public.
Track corrigenda and re-tender events.
Later: vendor concentration and repeat-award patterns, with strong methodological disclaimers.
19. Parliamentary and legislative accountability
Parliament Digital Library exposes questions and debates with member/ministry metadata, enabling structured participation analysis. [26] PRS provides state-legislature research and performance summaries where official assembly data is hard to normalize. [27]
Questions asked / answered by ministry and topic.
Debate participation and topic clusters.
Committee participation where data is available.
Attendance with denominator and session period.
Bills/private members’ business where comparable.
For MLAs, clearly distinguish official assembly data from third-party reconstructed data. The product should show a source badge on every metric.

23. UX principles
Plain-language headline; exact number underneath.
Never hide source and date behind a generic “data” label.
Use color for status, not political party identity.
Avoid red/green “good/bad” without context.
Every ranking has a “Why this rank?” drawer.
Every claim has “Source / period / definition / last update”.
Mobile-first because local discovery and evidence capture will often happen on phones.
Hindi-first and multilingual architecture; start with English + Hindi, add state languages based on adoption.
24. Architecture (proposed)
A source-first data platform is preferable to a traditional CRUD application. Suggested high-level components:
Source registry + scheduler: tracks portals, APIs, refresh cadence, owners and parser versions.
Raw evidence lake: immutable documents, CSVs, HTML snapshots and images.
Normalization layer: canonical schemas for people, places, institutions, funds and works.
Entity resolution service: name aliases, IDs, geography crosswalks, historical changes.
Graph / relationship store: official <-> role <-> geography <-> fund <-> work <-> institution <-> evidence.
Analytical store: precomputed metrics, comparisons and time-series.
Search index: fast name/place/project discovery.
API layer: web/mobile clients plus public read-only API later.
Moderation and provenance service: citizen evidence, reports and corrections.
24.1 Suggested stack
A practical implementation could use PostgreSQL + PostGIS for canonical data/geography, object storage for source documents/images, a search index such as OpenSearch/Elastic or PostgreSQL search initially, a graph representation implemented in PostgreSQL relational edges first and a graph database later only if needed, Python/TypeScript ingestion workers, and a Next.js web frontend. The exact stack is intentionally not locked by this PRD.

27. Legal, safety, and trust requirements
Use public-source material with source attribution and preserve original context.
Do not republish private contact details or sensitive personal information unnecessarily.
Criminal-case data must be described as declared/alleged/under trial unless a final judicial status is established by the source; never write “criminal” as an unqualified label.
Citizen submissions need defamation, harassment, doxxing and political-manipulation controls.
Provide correction/appeal mechanisms for officials and citizens.
Store a source version so corrections are auditable.
Avoid using the platform to target protected groups or voters with political persuasion.
Use “public accountability record” language rather than accusatory “expose” language.

31. Release acceptance criteria
For a supported MP, at least 95% of displayed financial figures must have a traceable primary source and reporting period.
Every project must show the source attribution relationship that connects it to the representative or institution.
No UI string may label jurisdiction-wide expenditure as “spent by politician” unless the source says so.
Users can reach a state -> district -> rural/urban local body path without knowing government terminology.
Every comparison view displays data coverage and the measurement definition.
Historical tenure and boundary changes do not silently rewrite old records.
Official data and citizen-submitted evidence are visually separated.
Broken or stale sources are surfaced to internal operations and reflected in freshness indicators.
At least one end-to-end trace works: fund release -> work -> location -> source document -> project status.

34. High-value features to add beyond the original concept
“Where the money went” map: all major publicly identifiable works around a user’s area, not only politician-linked works.
“Who is responsible?” resolver: project -> recommender -> sanctioning body -> implementing department -> elected institution.
Budget vs outcome view: planned vs actual expenditure plus published physical outputs.
Work timeline: every change in status or amount, not a static completion badge.
Data coverage meter: tell users when the evidence base is weak.
Budget calendar: remind users when central/state/municipal budgets and annual plans update.
Ward/village issue map: cluster reported public-service problems without turning the product into an unverified complaint board.
Project similarity: compare similar projects by cost, sector and geography to spot unusual patterns for human review.
Claim checker: later, parse a public speech/press release and map a claim to government records, clearly separating “claim” from “fact”.
Public-source citation pack: one click export with source URLs, reporting dates and methodology for journalists.
Open data API: downstream civic-tech ecosystem can build on normalized records.
Area watch: notify when a new project, sanction, tender, audit or completion record appears for a chosen area.
35. What the dashboard should feel like
The home experience should not resemble a political leaderboard. It should feel like a public “control room” for a place. A strong home screen can show:
“Your area” at the top with state/district/ULB/GP and overlapping constituencies.
Public money cards: released / sanctioned / spent for clearly defined funding streams.
Project progress strip: active / completed / stalled / unknown.
Map with real works, not generic political markers.
“What changed recently” timeline.
“Evidence quality” indicator.
Representative cards with role-specific metrics—not one score.
Quick compare.
The visual language should communicate public records, infrastructure and transparency rather than partisan politics: neutral status colors, restrained use of party colors, and strong source/methodology affordances.
36. Recommended first prototype
The highest-value prototype is not a national dashboard. It is one complete, high-trust journey that works exceptionally well for a single MP constituency and one adjacent MLA area. Prototype the following:
Search a village/city.
Resolve LGD geography + MP + MLA + ULB/GP.
Show the public money streams relevant to that place.
Open a project.
Show who recommended it, who sanctioned it, who executed it, what was sanctioned/spent, where it is, whether it is complete, and the source.
Compare the representative with one peer using normalized metrics.
Show data coverage and source freshness.
Let the citizen submit a correction/evidence note.
Once this money-trail interaction is trustworthy, the rest of the platform becomes a scaling problem rather than a conceptual problem.

38. Final product recommendation
Build Jan Nigrani as an “India Public Accountability Graph”, not as a politician rating app. The strongest product loop is: place -> representatives/institutions -> funding -> project -> responsibility -> evidence -> comparison. The app can later become a full government-performance layer, but credibility must be earned through precise attribution and source provenance first.
The most defensible product language is: “See what public money was allocated, where it went, what work was recorded, who had responsibility, and what evidence exists.” That promise is both useful to citizens and compatible with the way India’s government systems actually work.
